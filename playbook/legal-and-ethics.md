# Legal and ethical limits

Read this before you send anything. These are the lines that protect your
business and the people you contact.

## You are not a lawyer

Never tell a prospect they are "in violation", "non-compliant", or "will be
sued". You are not qualified to make those calls and it exposes you.

Say instead:
- "Your site has {N} issues that fail WCAG 2.1 AA."
- "These are the categories most commonly cited in accessibility complaints."
- "For advice on your specific legal exposure, you'd want your counsel."

The generated report already carries a disclaimer to this effect. Leave it in.

## Do not manufacture fear

Real urgency exists — the DOJ Title II deadlines are real, demand letters are
real. That is enough. Do not invent lawsuit statistics, do not imply you know
of a complaint against them, and do not use fake deadlines to force a decision.

Beyond being wrong, it is bad business: fear-closed clients churn, dispute
invoices, and leave reviews saying they were pressured.

## Cold email rules

### United States (CAN-SPAM)
Commercial email to businesses is lawful if you:
- Use accurate From, Reply-To, and subject lines — no deception
- Include a valid physical postal address
- Provide a clear opt-out and **honour it within 10 business days**
- Do not use harvested or scraped-by-bot address lists

### EU / UK (GDPR + PECR)
Materially stricter. You need a lawful basis; legitimate interest can apply for
B2B outreach but must be documented and balanced. Corporate role addresses
(`info@`, `contact@`) are more defensible than named individuals. Provide
opt-out and identify yourself fully.

### Canada (CASL)
Among the strictest regimes, with meaningful penalties. Requires consent —
express or implied — and implied consent is narrower than most people assume.
Research it properly before emailing Canadian organisations.

### Regardless of jurisdiction
- Honour every opt-out immediately and permanently
- Three emails maximum, then stop
- No misleading subject lines ("Re:" on a first contact is a lie)
- Never scrape addresses behind a login or in breach of a site's terms

## Scanning other people's websites

Scanning a public page is ordinary browser behaviour — you are loading pages
the way any visitor does. Stay on the right side of it:

- **Public pages only.** Never log in, never scan authenticated areas, without
  written permission.
- **Keep the volume gentle.** The default settings are polite. Do not raise
  concurrency to hammer a small server.
- **Honour robots.txt** where a site asks not to be crawled.
- **Never test for vulnerabilities.** You are checking accessibility. Probing
  for security flaws without authorisation is an entirely different act with
  entirely different consequences.
- **Stop if asked.** Immediately, and remove them from your list.

## Do not sell accessibility overlays

Overlay widgets that claim one-line compliance have been named in accessibility
lawsuits, and disability advocacy organisations are broadly critical of them.
They do not fix the underlying markup.

Selling one would make you the problem you are claiming to solve.

## Deliver what you sold

- Do not promise "full compliance" — you cannot guarantee it, and neither can
  anyone else
- Do not claim a certification you do not hold
- Do not take remediation work you cannot deliver; subcontract openly instead
- If you get something wrong, fix it at your cost and say so plainly

## Why this matters beyond liability

The people affected by these defects are real. Someone cannot book a dental
appointment because a form field has no label. That is the actual problem, and
fixing it is the actual product.

Businesses built on that hold up. Businesses built on manufactured panic do not.
