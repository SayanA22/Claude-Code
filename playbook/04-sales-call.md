# The call

Twenty to thirty minutes. Your job is to find out whether they have a real
problem you can solve, not to convince anyone of anything.

## Structure

**1. Open (2 min)** — "Thanks for making time. Before I walk through the
report, tell me a bit about the site — who looks after it, and has
accessibility come up before?"

Then be quiet. What they say next tells you the budget, the decision-maker, and
the urgency.

**2. Walk the report (8 min)** — Share your screen. Three findings, no more.
For each: what it is, who it locks out, what fixing it involves. Skip the rule
IDs entirely.

**3. Ask the questions that matter (5 min)**
- "Has anyone raised a complaint about the site being hard to use?"
- "Do you have a contract or grant that requires WCAG conformance?"
- "Who'd need to sign off on fixing this?"
- "Is there budget allocated, or would this need to be found?"

**4. Recommend one thing (3 min)** — Not a menu. "Based on what I'm seeing,
I'd start with the critical items — that's the Fix It package. I'll send scope
and pricing today."

**5. Close on a next step (2 min)** — "I'll have that over by end of day. Can
we get fifteen minutes Thursday to go through it?" A scheduled follow-up, not
"let me know."

## Never quote on the call

You have not read the findings closely enough yet. "I'll send exact scope and
pricing today" costs you nothing and protects your margin.

## Objections

**"How much?"** (asked early)
> "Depends on scope — between {low} and {high} for a site this size. Let me
> look properly and send you an exact number today."

**"We'll get our web developer to fix it."**
> "That's completely reasonable — the report is built to be worked from
> directly, so hand it straight over. Two things worth knowing: about a third
> of WCAG is machine-checkable, so there's more that needs a manual pass, and
> fixes tend to regress without re-testing. If it'd help to have someone verify
> their work afterwards, that's a smaller engagement I can do."

**"Can't we just use an accessibility overlay?"**
> "I'd genuinely advise against it. Overlay widgets have been named in
> accessibility lawsuits rather than preventing them, and the disability
> community is broadly critical of them. They don't fix the underlying markup —
> they layer over it. You'd be paying a subscription for something that doesn't
> address what's in this report."

*This objection is common and you should be firm on it. It is also the fastest
way to establish that you know the field.*

**"We don't have budget this quarter."**
> "Understood. Can I ask — is there a compliance deadline driving this, or is
> it more on the radar for later? If it's later, I'll send the report over and
> follow up next quarter. Nothing in it expires."

**"Is this actually a legal requirement for us?"**
> "I'm not a lawyer and I can't advise you on that — genuinely. What I can tell
> you is what the technical standard is and where your site sits against it.
> ada.gov is the primary source, and for anything about your specific exposure
> you'd want your counsel."

*Never bluff this one. Confident legal claims from a non-lawyer are how you get
into trouble, and experienced buyers can tell.*

**"Send me a proposal."** (with no engagement)
> "Happy to — one question first so it's actually useful: is this something
> you're looking to move on this quarter, or gathering information for later?"

Distinguishes a real opportunity from a polite exit.

## After the call — same day

Send: scope, fixed price, timeline, what's included, what's excluded,
deposit terms. One page. If it needs more than one page, your scope is unclear.

Then follow up on day 3 and day 7. Most deals close on the second follow-up,
and most people never send it.
