The problem

A bill nobody can read, and a right nobody uses

A hospital bill arrives as a wall of five-digit codes. Almost no one can tell whether a line is correct, whether the price is defensible, or whether federal law already voids it. So the bill gets paid, or it goes to collections.

Three specific things go wrong on a bill, and they need three different arguments:

Coding errors — a procedure keyed twice, a panel billed alongside the tests it already contains, twenty units of a drug given once. These are factually wrong and get removed.
Prices — a $612 ankle X-ray that Medicare pays $40 for. Not illegal, but every hospital has been required since 2021 to publish its own cash price, which makes the number negotiable.
Voided charges — an out-of-network ER physician's balance bill, barred outright by the No Surprises Act; a bill $400 over a good faith estimate, which opens a federal dispute; a preventive screening billed with cost sharing, barred by the ACA.

Three tiers of noncustomers

The market is everyone who has already given up

Bill advocacy today serves a sliver: large bills, patients who will hand over their records and a third of the winnings. Itemize is aimed at the three tiers outside that sliver.

1

Soon-to-be: the ones who suspect, then stop

They open the bill, feel that something is off, search a phrase from it, land on forum threads and a paywall, and pay. They are already looking for exactly this and finding nothing usable.

Reached at the moment of doubt — search, and the hospital's own financial counselor.
2

Refusing: the ones an advocate turns away

Their bill is $700, not $70,000, so no contingency advocate will take the case — the fee would not cover the hour. Others refuse on principle to sign over a third of their own money or hand a stranger their medical records.

Small bills are the majority of bills. Nobody is serving them at all.
3

Unexplored: the ones who never knew a bill could be wrong

Uninsured and self-pay patients, students paying a first ER bill, people billing in a second language, anyone who pays the day it arrives because a hospital bill reads like a tax notice. Their legal rights — a good faith estimate, a $400 variance dispute, a collections hold — expire unused.

This tier has never been anyone's customer, and it is the largest of the three.
Strategy canvas

Where the curve breaks away

Advocacy firms compete on expertise and charge for it. The status quo — an insurer portal, a summary of benefits, a call to the billing office — competes on nothing. Itemize takes the factors both under-serve and raises them, while cutting the two that make advocacy expensive: expert labor and case intake.

Eliminate · Reduce · Raise · Create

What comes out, and what goes in

The grid is the whole business in one page: the left column is how the price gets to zero, the right column is why anyone would use it anyway.

Eliminate

  • The contingency fee. No 25–35% of savings. The patient keeps everything.
  • Case intake. No consultation call, no case manager, no queue.
  • The records handover. No account, no upload, no third party holding your medical history.
  • The minimum bill size. No threshold below which the case is not worth taking.

Reduce

  • Time to a first answer — from weeks of back-and-forth to about three minutes.
  • Documents the patient must gather — the itemized bill alone starts the audit.
  • Jargon. Every finding is written in the sentence you would actually say on the phone.
  • Marginal cost per audit — to effectively zero, which is what makes free durable.

Raise

  • Legal coverage. The No Surprises Act, good faith estimates, ACA preventive rules and price transparency, all applied to the same bill at once.
  • Price transparency. Every coded line benchmarked against the Medicare rate, with the multiple stated out loud.
  • Evidence. Each objection carries the rule it rests on, so the billing office cannot wave it off.
  • Privacy. The audit runs in the browser; no bill ever reaches a server.

Create

  • An instant line-by-line verdict read off the bill itself, with a dollar figure attached to every objection.
  • The appeal packet — a formatted letter and a phone script, written before you have decided to fight.
  • A rights engine that follows the patient: change coverage from self-pay to insured and the governing law, and the argument, changes with it.
  • An audit worth running on a $600 bill — a category that did not previously exist.

$0 — and the growth engine

Why the unit economics hold The audit is a deterministic rules engine running in the visitor's own browser over public reference data. There is no inference cost, no storage cost, and no per-audit staffing cost — one audit and one million audits cost the same to serve. That is the low-cost half of value innovation, and it is what lets the differentiated half be given away.

Technical feasibility

Built, not imagined

The prototype linked at the top is real and runs today. It parses an itemized bill, applies ten rules across coding, pricing and federal patient-protection law, caps overlapping findings so the headline number cannot be inflated, and drafts the appeal letter and phone script. Everything it needs already exists in public:

Deliberately, the core has no language model in it. Rules are auditable, repeatable and explainable to a billing office — and they cost nothing per run, which is the entire reason the product can be free.

Honest limits

What would have to go right

Reference data at scale. The prototype ships rounded Medicare rates for a demonstration set of codes. Production needs the full fee schedules and thousands of hospital price files ingested and kept current — a real engineering job, though every input is free and machine-readable.

Reading real bills. Bills arrive as paper and PDFs in hundreds of layouts. The parser handles clean text today; photographs need OCR and a correction step where the patient confirms what was read.

Staying on the right side of the line. Itemize gives patients information and drafts their own letters. It does not represent anyone, and it says so on every screen. Anything resembling representation would need licensed advocates — and would rebuild the cost structure this strategy exists to eliminate.

Hospitals may not like it. They do not have to. The patient is the buyer, the arguments cite the hospital's own published prices and federal rules, and a billing office that ignores a documented dispute is a billing office in front of a regulator.
